\title{Password-Derived Key Image Encryption Using Frequency-Domain Convolution: Analysis and Performance Evaluation}

\author{M.C. Zuriel L\'opez Sosa \and Dra. B\'arbara Emma S\'anchez Rinza \and

\institute{Benemerita Universidad Autonoma de Puebla\\

\begin{abstract}
Image encryption is essential for secure digital communication and the
protection of visual data. This paper presents an encryption method based
on the Fourier Transform (FT), in which both the original image and a key,
cryptographically generated from a password, are transformed into the
frequency domain. The key is derived using PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 and expanded
with SHAKE-256 to exactly the size of the image, removing the need for an
external key file. The encryption process applies the Fast Fourier
Transform (FFT) to both representations and multiplies them element-wise;
the encrypted image is reconstructed using the Inverse FFT (IFFT) and
stored as a 16-bit-per-channel PNG with the normalization metadata embedded
in the file. To evaluate the security and effectiveness of the method,
statistical analyses (histograms, entropy, and PSNR) and robustness tests
against Gaussian noise, resizing, and incorrect-password attacks were
performed. The results show that the encrypted image exhibits high
randomness and low correlation with the original, that decryption with the
correct password recovers the original image exactly (infinite PSNR), and
that the method is highly sensitive to alterations of the key, the size of
the encrypted image, and the password used.

\keywords{Image encryption, Fourier Transform, digital security,
frequency-domain encryption, key derivation, PBKDF2, SHAKE-256,
cryptographic analysis.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In the digital age, information security is a priority, particularly
regarding the protection of images, which often contain sensitive data.
Digital images, due to their visual nature, present an additional
challenge for traditional spatial-domain encryption methods.
Frequency-domain encryption techniques, such as those based on the Fourier
Transform (FT), have gained attention for their ability to effectively
conceal data patterns, making decryption without the proper key difficult
\cite{refregier,unnikrishnan}. Existing alternatives include random phase
encoding schemes in the optical domain \cite{javidi} and chaos-based
schemes \cite{chaos1,chaos2}, which pursue similar randomization goals
through nonlinear spatial-domain dynamics.

Image encryption based on FT, and specifically on the Fast Fourier
Transform (FFT), offers an efficient solution to the problem of protecting
images over unsecured channels. This work presents a method that
transforms both the original image and a key into the frequency domain and
combines them through element-wise multiplication. Unlike schemes that
require distributing a separate key image alongside the encrypted data, the
key used here is derived from a \textbf{password} using standard
cryptographic functions, so that only the encrypted file and the password
are needed to recover the original image, in line with the formal
cryptographic security notions requiring that an attacker without the
correct key be unable to distinguish the ciphertext from random noise
\cite{bellare}.

The aim of this paper is to present and evaluate this method, examining its
effectiveness and security. Performance evaluation is conducted through
statistical tests (histogram comparison, entropy, and PSNR) and robustness
tests against potential attacks.

The structure of this article is as follows: Section 2 describes the
proposed algorithm, detailing the encryption and decryption process.
Section 3 presents the tests performed and the analysis of results.
Finally, Section 4 concludes with a discussion of the findings.

\section{Methodology}

The algorithm is based on the \textbf{Fourier Transform convolution
property}, which states that the Fourier transform of the convolution of
two functions equals the product of their respective Fourier transforms:
\[
\mathcal{F}(f * g) = \mathcal{F}(f) \cdot \mathcal{F}(g)
\]
Isolating the convolution term and applying the Inverse Fourier Transform
($\mathcal{F}^{-1}$) on both sides gives:
\[
f * g = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}(f) \cdot \mathcal{F}(g)\right)
\]
This implies that convolution in the spatial domain can be computed
indirectly through multiplication in the frequency domain, followed by the
Inverse Transform. In practice, both transforms are computed using the Fast
Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm \cite{cooley}, which reduces the
computational complexity from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N\log N)$.

Throughout this article, $f$ denotes the \textbf{original image} to be
encrypted and $g$ denotes the \textbf{key image}. Encryption is defined as
the convolution $f * g$: this convolution, computed in the frequency domain
as described above, \textbf{is} the encrypted image. To recover the
original image, it suffices to solve for $f$ in the convolution property,
dividing in the frequency domain by $\mathcal{F}(g)$ instead of
multiplying:
\[
f = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}(f * g)}{\mathcal{F}(g)}\right)
\]
This is precisely the relationship instantiated by the encryption process
(Section~2.2) and the decryption process (Section~2.3), respectively: the
former produces $f*g$ from $f$ and $g$; the latter recovers $f$ from $f*g$
and $g$. Since the images used are in RGB format, all operations are
performed independently on each of the three color channels (Red, Green,
and Blue), i.e., $f$, $g$, and $f*g$ are handled channel by channel.

\subsection{Deriving the key $g$ from a password}

Instead of requiring $g$ to come from an external key image, $g$ is
cryptographically generated from a password. This design follows
Kerckhoffs's principle \cite{kerckhoffs}, which states that the security of
a cryptographic system should rely solely on the secrecy of the key (here,
the password) and not on the secrecy of the algorithm or the possession of
an additional file. The derivation follows the same principle used by
modern stream ciphers and by password-based key derivation functions
\cite{nist800132,scrypt}: deriving a short key and expanding it into a
pseudorandom stream of the required length.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A random 16-byte salt is generated at encryption time.
  \item The password and salt are processed with
    \textbf{PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256} \cite{pbkdf2} using 310{,}000 iterations,
    producing 32 derived bytes.
  \item Those 32 bytes are expanded with \textbf{SHAKE-256} (an
    extendable-output function from the SHA-3 family \cite{sha3}) to obtain
    exactly $\text{height}\times\text{width}\times 3$ bytes: these bytes
    constitute $g$, generated at exactly $f$'s dimensions, without
    requiring any resizing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Encryption process: obtaining $f*g$}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inputs}: the original image $f$ to encrypt and a password.
  \item \textbf{Key derivation}: $g$ is obtained as described above.
  \item \textbf{Fourier Transform}: the FFT is applied to each channel of
    $f$ and of $g$, giving $\mathcal{F}(f)$ and $\mathcal{F}(g)$.
  \item \textbf{Element-wise multiplication}: $\mathcal{F}(f)\cdot\mathcal{F}(g)$
    is computed.
  \item \textbf{Inverse Fourier Transform}: the IFFT is applied to the
    previous result, yielding $f*g$ in the spatial domain.
  \item \textbf{Normalization and storage}: per-channel Min-Max
    normalization is applied to $f*g$ to scale its values to a 16-bit range
    ($[0,65535]$), since the convolution result has a much larger value
    range than a conventional image. The minimum and maximum used, together
    with the salt and iteration count, are embedded as metadata inside the
    encrypted PNG file itself (as a JSON-encoded \texttt{tEXt} chunk), so
    that the resulting file --- which stores $f*g$ quantized to 16 bits ---
    is self-contained. This file is the \textbf{encrypted image}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Decryption process: recovering $f$ from $f*g$}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inputs}: the encrypted image (storing $f*g$ quantized to 16
    bits) and the password.
  \item \textbf{Reading metadata}: the salt, iteration count, and per-channel
    min/max ranges are read from the file itself.
  \item \textbf{Key re-derivation}: the process from Section~2.1 is repeated
    with the same salt, regenerating $g$ identically to the one used during
    encryption.
  \item \textbf{Reversing normalization}: the 16-bit values are converted
    back to the real scale of $f*g$ using the stored ranges.
  \item \textbf{Fourier Transform}: the FFT is applied to $f*g$ (now
    denormalized) and to $g$, giving $\mathcal{F}(f*g)$ and
    $\mathcal{F}(g)$.
  \item \textbf{Element-wise division}: $f$ is recovered by dividing both
    frequency-domain representations:
    \[
    f \approx \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}(f * g)}{\mathcal{F}(g) + \epsilon}\right)
    \]
    with $\epsilon = 1\times10^{-10}$ to avoid division by zero. The result
    of this operation is the \textbf{decrypted image}.
  \item \textbf{Rounding and clipping}: the result is rounded and clipped
    to the valid $[0,255]$ range to obtain the final 8-bit image.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting storage format corresponds to a 16-bit-per-channel image,
capable of representing the full range of values required by the
normalization described above.

\section{Analysis and Performance Evaluation}

This section presents the analysis and performance evaluation of the
proposed method. The evaluation was performed on a $3024\times4032$-pixel
test photograph and consists of three stages: visual inspection,
statistical analysis, and robustness testing against attacks.

The encrypted image $f*g$ shows no recognizable trace of $f$'s content,
while the decrypted image is, as quantified in Section~3.3, identical to
$f$.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

\subsubsection{Histogram analysis.}
Intensity histograms are a standard tool for analyzing the pixel-value
distribution of an image \cite{gonzalez}. Histograms of $f$ and $f*g$ were
computed for each color channel (Fig.~2). The original image's histogram shows pronounced
peaks associated with regions of uniform color (sky, skin, clothing). The
encrypted image's histogram, in contrast, takes on a smooth, unimodal,
Gaussian-like shape, consistent with the central limit theorem applied to
the convolution of two statistically independent signals: the peaked
structure of the original disappears entirely, indicating that the
encryption process effectively conceals the original intensity
distribution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{hist_original.png}\\[4pt]
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{hist_encrypted.png}
  \caption{Per-channel (R, G, B) histograms of the original image (top) and
  the encrypted image (bottom).}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\subsubsection{Entropy analysis.}
The average Shannon entropy \cite{shannon} (across the three channels) of
$f$ is $7.6512$, while that of $f*g$ is $6.7715$. This
slight decrease, also observed in other FT-based encryption schemes, is
explained by the fact that Min-Max normalization concentrates values into
a bell-shaped distribution rather than uniformly saturating all 256
possible levels; nonetheless, entropy remains high, confirming that the
encryption produces an image with high apparent randomness.

\subsubsection{PSNR evaluation.}
The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) \cite{psnr} between $f$ and the image
recovered by decrypting $f*g$ with the correct password was computed. The result
obtained was:
\[
\text{MSE} = 0.0 \qquad \text{PSNR} = \infty
\]
That is, the recovered image is \textbf{bit-for-bit identical} to the
original. This confirms that, by using 16 bits per channel with the
normalization ranges stored as exact metadata, the encryption and
decryption process introduces no information loss whatsoever.

\subsection{Robustness against attacks}

Beyond histogram, entropy, and PSNR testing, the image encryption
literature frequently evaluates pixel-level sensitivity through randomness
tests such as NPCR and UACI \cite{npcr}. In this work, robustness was
evaluated directly against three concrete attacks: Gaussian noise,
resizing, and an incorrect password.

\subsubsection{Gaussian noise attack.}
Gaussian noise was added directly to $f*g$ (the 16-bit encrypted data), at three
standard deviation ($\sigma$) levels expressed as a fraction of the 16-bit
range ($0.05\%$, $0.25\%$, and $0.5\%$, equivalent to
$\sigma \approx 32.8$, $163.8$, and $327.7$ in 16-bit units). The results
are shown in Fig.~3 and Table~1.

As expected, PSNR decreases as the noise level increases. However, even at
the highest level tested, the recovered image remains clearly recognizable
(Fig.~3), indicating that the method reasonably tolerates small
perturbations of the encrypted file without losing the usefulness of the
recovered image.

\subsubsection{Resizing attack.}
The effect of applying bilinear interpolation to change the scale of
$f*g$ (16-bit) before decryption was evaluated (regenerating $g$ at the
new size in each case, since $g$ is deterministically derived from the
password and the image's dimensions).

\emph{Downscaling.} $f*g$ was reduced to 1\%, 5\%, and 10\%
of its original size. In all three cases, decryption resulted in a
\textbf{fully saturated} image (every pixel white, value 255; Fig.~4). This
is explained by the fact that the zero-frequency term (the DC component)
of the encrypted data retains a magnitude associated with the original
image size, while the key is regenerated at a much smaller scale; the
mismatch between these magnitudes causes the frequency-domain division
result to overflow and saturate when clipped to the $[0,255]$ range.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{grid_downscale.png}
  \caption{Decryption result after reducing the encrypted image to 1\%, 5\%,
  and 10\% of its original size: total saturation (white) in all three
  cases.}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\emph{Upscaling.} The encrypted image was enlarged by 1\%, 5\%, and 10\%.
In all three cases, the result was completely unrecognizable colored noise
(Fig.~5), with no trace of the original content. Interpolation introduces
artifacts that disrupt the frequency-domain representation, preventing
correct decryption.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{grid_upscale.png}
  \caption{Decryption result after enlarging the encrypted image by 1\%,
  5\%, and 10\%: unrecognizable noise in all three cases.}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

In both cases (downscaling and upscaling), the method proves to be highly
sensitive to any geometric alteration of the encrypted file, a desirable
property for detecting unauthorized tampering.

\subsubsection{Decryption with an incorrect password.}
Decryption was attempted using a password different from the one used
during encryption. The result, shown in Fig.~6, is completely
unrecognizable colored noise:
\[
\text{MSE} = 13727.85 \qquad \text{PSNR} = 6.75~\text{dB}
\]

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.32\textwidth]{eval_wrong_password.jpg}
  \caption{Result of attempting to decrypt the image with an incorrect
  password.}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

This result confirms that the scheme critically depends on the exact
password: even a different password produces a completely unusable
reconstruction, reinforcing the security of the method.

\section{Conclusions}

The analysis demonstrates that the proposed frequency-domain convolution
encryption method, with a password-derived key, effectively transforms an
image's content into a randomized-looking representation that is difficult
to distinguish, visually and statistically, from noise. Statistical
evaluations confirm high entropy and a complete loss of the original
histogram structure in the encrypted image, while decryption with the
correct password recovers the original image \textbf{exactly} (infinite
PSNR), unlike equivalent schemes based on 8-bit normalization, which
introduce irreversible information loss.

Robustness tests show that the method tolerates moderate levels of
Gaussian noise while keeping the recovered image recognizable, but is
extremely sensitive to geometric transformations (resizing) and to any
deviation in the password used, both desirable properties for detecting
unauthorized tampering with the encrypted file. Overall, the scheme offers
a strong level of security while also removing the need to distribute a
separate key file, making it practical for secure image transmission.
